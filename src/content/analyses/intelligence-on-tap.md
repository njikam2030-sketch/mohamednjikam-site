Last Tuesday, a single room in Washington held more of the world's future than most parliaments.

On 29 September, President Trump hosted the leaders of the AI industry at the White House: Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Sundar Pichai, Jensen Huang, Jeff Bezos, OpenAI's Greg Brockman and others. They left having signed a voluntary accord to police themselves. A few days earlier, many of the same people had sat at the state dinner for China's President Xi.

Read those two scenes together and you see the century we have entered. Intelligence is no longer just a technology. It has become a matter of state, a matter of geopolitics, and very soon, a monthly bill.

## AI is becoming the new electricity

A hundred years ago, a factory without electricity was not a slightly less efficient factory. It was a dead factory. Twenty years ago, a business without internet access was not old-fashioned. It was invisible.

AI is following the same path, only faster. Within a decade, I believe access to intelligence will be a utility, like power, water or broadband. Every household, every company and every government will pay a subscription for it. You will not ask whether your business uses AI, any more than you ask today whether it uses electricity. You will ask how much it consumes, at what price, and from whom.

The unit of this new economy is already here: the token. Tokens are to intelligence what kilowatt-hours are to electricity. Every email drafted, every contract reviewed, every credit analysed and every line of code written by a machine is measured, priced and billed in tokens.

## The new divide: those who have tokens, and those who do not

Here is the uncomfortable part. Electricity was eventually distributed to almost everyone. Intelligence may not be, at least not equally.

In the coming decades, the advantage of a company, a city or a country will depend on how much intelligence it can afford to consume. A firm with a large token budget will analyse more, test more, serve more clients and move faster than a firm rationing every query. The token budget will sit next to payroll and rent on every income statement, and for many businesses, it will become the largest line.

Those who control the production of intelligence, the chips, the data centres and the energy that feeds them, will hold a position comparable to the oil majors of the twentieth century. That is why a lunch at the White House matters to an entrepreneur in Douala, Lagos or London.

## The money behind the machine

This revolution is also a financial one. The data centres, power plants and chips behind AI require capital on a scale the world has rarely seen. The Institute of International Finance identified AI infrastructure, alongside emerging markets and defence spending, as one of the forces behind the latest record in global debt, now above $365 trillion.

We are financing a new industrial base, largely with borrowed money. As with railways and electricity before it, some of this investment will build lasting wealth, and some will end in painful corrections. The financiers who understand which is which will shape the next decades.

## What happens to work

I do not believe AI will end work. I believe it will change what we are paid for.

Tasks that can be described can increasingly be delegated: drafting, summarising, calculating, translating, checking. What remains scarce, and therefore valuable, is what cannot be easily described: judgement, trust, relationships, accountability and the courage to decide. A machine can prepare ten financing structures in a minute. Choosing the right one, and convincing a bank and a client to sign it, remains deeply human.

Every professional will soon work with a team of AI assistants, paid for by subscription, the way we pay today for a phone and a laptop. The question for each of us is simple: will we manage that team, or be replaced by someone who does?

## Even the builders want to slow down

What struck me most about the White House meeting was not who was there, but why. In recent weeks, Dario Amodei called on companies to "pace the frontier" of the most advanced models, and Sam Altman and Elon Musk agreed with calls for an industry-wide slowdown.

When the people building a technology ask for brakes, the rest of us should listen. Power on this scale, concentrated in a handful of companies and a few countries, raises questions that markets alone will not answer: who sets the rules, who audits the systems, and who decides what intelligence costs.

## What it means for Africa

Africa cannot afford to be only a consumer of intelligence, paying for it in dollars and importing it like fuel. We have done this before with energy and paid the price.

But Africa also has a history of leapfrogging. We went straight to mobile phones without building landlines, and straight to mobile money without building bank branches in every village. The same can happen with AI, if we invest in energy, data centres, skills and the financing that makes them possible.

This is where finance becomes strategy. The countries and companies that secure capital for this infrastructure today will own a share of the meter tomorrow. The others will simply pay the bill.

## Who will own the meter?

Every great utility of the past century created two kinds of people: those who owned the network, and those who paid for access. AI will do the same, at a speed and scale we have never experienced.

> The next decades will not be decided by who uses AI. Everyone will. They will be decided by who produces it, who finances it, and who sets the price of a thought.

I know which side I want my companies, my clients and my continent to be on.
