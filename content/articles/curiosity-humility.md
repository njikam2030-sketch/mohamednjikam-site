Ask what makes a good finance leader and the answers are usually technical: accounting, modelling, treasury, negotiation. All of them matter. None is enough.

Years as an analyst and then as a chief financial officer have persuaded me that two habits count for more over a career than any technical skill. They are curiosity and humility. Both sound soft. In practice they are disciplines, and they decide whether a finance function protects a company or merely reports on it.

## A number is a question

Every finance team produces numbers. Remarkably few interrogate them. A margin falls by two points and the easy explanation is that costs went up. The curious response is to ask which costs, for which products and customers, starting when, and why then. The answer is nearly always more specific and more useful than anyone expected: a supplier changed its terms, a currency moved, a sales team discounted to hit a quarterly target.

> A number is not an answer. It is the beginning of a question.

Curiosity is what turns finance from bookkeeping into intelligence. The CFO who understands why the numbers move can warn the chief executive before a problem shows up in the accounts. The one who merely reports them arrives after the damage is done.

The habit should extend well beyond the company's walls. Interest rates, energy prices, exchange rates and regulation all reach the cash flows eventually, and a curious finance leader reads widely: central bank minutes, bond market commentary, trade data, the annual reports of competitors and suppliers. This is not an academic pastime. When gilt yields jumped this October, the companies that had already shortened their payment cycles and fixed part of their debt were those whose leaders had been paying attention months before.

## Designing for being wrong

Humility in finance is not modesty. It is a design principle. Every forecast is wrong; the only questions are by how much and in which direction. A humble finance leader accepts this and builds decisions that survive error: liquidity buffers, staged investments, exit options, covenants with room to breathe and scenarios that include outcomes nobody in the room wants to discuss.

Markets punish overconfidence with remarkable consistency. Warren Buffett's remark that you only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out is really an observation about humility. The people caught out are seldom those who lacked information. They are those who were too sure.

The costliest mistakes I have seen did not come from ignorance. There was the acquisition that "could not fail", paid for with debt that left no margin; the currency exposure left unhedged because the rate had been stable for years; the project approved because the most senior person in the room wanted it and nobody challenged the assumptions. In every case the information needed to avoid the error existed. What was missing was a willingness to say "I might be wrong" early enough to change course.

## Habits that can be learnt

Both qualities can be practised. Pushing past the first explanation of a variance, and the second, usually reveals the real cause two or three questions down. Writing beside every forecast the three assumptions that matter most, and what would prove them wrong, makes the forecast honest. A pre-mortem, in which a team imagines that a decision has failed a year from now and explains why, surfaces risks that optimism hides. Teams stop bringing bad news if the messenger is punished, so the messenger has to be thanked. Reading history, psychology and economics explains behaviour that spreadsheets cannot. And a decision journal, recording what was decided and what was expected, is the quickest way to learn where one's judgement is reliable and where it is not.

Teams take their cue from the top. A curious leader produces a team that investigates; a humble one produces a team that tells the truth. Together they create something every company needs and few possess: a finance function that sees problems early and says so plainly.

These habits are tested hardest in a crisis, when the pressure to appear certain is greatest. In a liquidity squeeze, the curious leader asks precise questions at once: which payments fall due this week, which customers are paying late and why, which credit lines can be drawn and on what terms. The answers turn panic into a plan. The humble leader, meanwhile, says what is known and what is not. Lenders, employees and boards can work with uncertainty. What destroys trust is false confidence later contradicted by events. "Here is what we know, here is what we are doing, here is when we will know more" buys the time needed to solve the problem.

## Survival skills

I write often about African economies, and there these habits matter in a particular way. Businesses on the continent contend with more volatile currencies, less predictable regulation and thinner capital markets than their peers in London or New York. In that setting, curiosity about detail and humility about forecasts are not refinements. They are survival skills. The African finance leaders I admire most tend to hold more liquidity than the textbooks recommend, hedge more carefully and question assumptions that would be taken for granted elsewhere. They are not pessimists. They are realists who have learnt that the unexpected is normal.

Technical skill makes a finance leader useful. Curiosity makes them perceptive. Humility keeps their company solvent. In a world of higher rates, faster technology and more frequent shocks, the last two are no longer optional extras. They are the job.
