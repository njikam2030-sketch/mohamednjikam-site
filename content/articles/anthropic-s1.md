For three years, the economics of frontier artificial intelligence have been argued over with very little evidence. The companies building the largest models were private, their accounts were confidential and the numbers that leaked were chosen by people with an interest in the story. On 26 October that changed. Anthropic filed its registration statement for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT, and for the first time investors can read the accounts of a frontier laboratory line by line.

The headline is extraordinary growth. Revenue in the first half of 2026 was about $16.3bn, against roughly $1.16bn a year earlier. The second quarter alone brought in about $11.6bn. Around four-fifths of it came from usage-based access to the company's models through its programming interface; subscriptions made up the rest. Fortune counts some 11,900 enterprise customers spending more than $100,000 a year.

This is the clearest confirmation yet of an argument I made in Intelligence on Tap: that intelligence is becoming a utility, sold by the unit and consumed like electricity. A business in which 80 per cent of revenue is metered consumption is not a software company in the old sense. It is closer to a power generator, whose income rises with every query its customers send.

## The loss that is not quite a loss

The second number will travel faster than the first. Anthropic reported a net loss of about $80.7bn for the half-year. Taken at face value, that would be one of the largest losses in corporate history. It is not what it seems. Most of it reflects changes in the fair value of convertible notes, an accounting consequence of the company's soaring valuation rather than cash leaving the building. On an adjusted basis, Fortune calculates operating income of about $1.62bn, a margin of roughly 10 per cent.

Investors should hold both numbers in mind. The adjusted figure suggests a business that can already cover its running costs. The headline figure is a reminder that the capital structure behind it is complex, and that the claims of earlier investors will weigh on the shares that public buyers acquire.

> The most important number in the filing is not revenue or profit. It is the $498bn the company has committed to spend on computing.

## Half a trillion dollars of promises

That figure, which Fortune highlights as the filing's asterisk, refers to non-cancellable commitments for computing capacity. It is the cost of the factory. Every model Anthropic sells is produced on chips and data centres that it rents from others under long contracts, and those contracts must be paid whether or not revenue keeps compounding.

This is where the prospectus connects to the credit story I described in Borrowing for Intelligence. The cloud providers that sell this capacity are financing their data centres with record bond issuance. Their confidence rests in part on contracts like these. The laboratory's commitments are the hyperscalers' revenue, and the hyperscalers' bonds are, indirectly, a claim on the laboratory's ability to keep growing. When analysts ask who bears the risk of the AI build-out, the answer is now written down: it is spread across a chain of companies, each of which is counting on the next.

## What the valuation assumes

The filing contains no price range; that will come in a later amendment. The last private round, in May, valued Anthropic at about $965bn, and press reports speak of a listing valuation between $1.5tn and $2tn. Even at the lower figure, investors would be paying between roughly 30 and 45 times annualised second-quarter revenue for a company whose adjusted margin is 10 per cent and whose largest costs are contracted for years ahead.

That may prove justified. Revenue growing fourteenfold in a year is not a number any serious analyst should dismiss, and the business is plainly meeting a real demand. But a valuation of that order assumes not only that growth continues for years, but that prices for intelligence hold up as competitors multiply, including Chinese laboratories offering capable models at far lower prices. The history of utilities is that the unit price falls over time and the winners are those with the lowest cost of production.

## Why it matters beyond Silicon Valley

The listing, if it proceeds on the scale expected, will cap a year in which American IPO proceeds have already set a record. It will also give the world a public, daily price for frontier AI, something that until now existed only in private rounds. Pension funds and index investors in London, Lagos and Johannesburg will end up owning a piece of it, whether they choose to or not.

For finance directors, the lesson is more immediate. The prospectus shows what their own AI budgets are paying for: a metered service whose provider is itself committed to enormous fixed costs. That gives buyers bargaining power, and it argues for contracts that track usage closely and avoid long commitments while prices are still falling.

The filing does not settle the argument about whether artificial intelligence is a bubble. It does something more useful. It turns a debate about belief into a debate about numbers, and the numbers say that the business of selling intelligence is real, fast-growing and extraordinarily capital-hungry. Investors should price all three.
