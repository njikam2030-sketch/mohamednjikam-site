The Federal Reserve ends its October meeting on Wednesday, and for once the decision itself is not the main event. As of 27 October, the CME FedWatch tool put the probability that the central bank would leave rates unchanged at close to 90 per cent. The question that will move markets is what the Fed says about December.

Six weeks ago the answer seemed clear. On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously, 12 to 0, to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point to 3.75-4 per cent, its first increase since 2023. Its projections showed 16 of 19 participants expecting at least one more rise this year. In late September, some analysts expected the next move as early as this week.

## Why the Fed may wait

Since then the picture has become more complicated. Higher oil prices are pushing up headline inflation, but they also act as a tax on consumers and businesses, slowing spending. Equity markets have been volatile, with losses in technology and AI-related shares. Long-term bond yields have risen, tightening financial conditions on their own and doing some of the Fed's work for it.

A central bank that has just resumed tightening after a long pause also has reasons to move carefully. Officials will want to see how the September increase feeds through, and the next consumer price report, due on 10 November, will be one of the last pieces of evidence before the December meeting.

> The Fed can afford to skip a meeting. What it cannot afford is to let markets conclude that one rise was the end.

## The case for December

The argument for another increase rests on the persistence of inflation. With Brent above $110 a barrel and disruption in the Strait of Hormuz showing no sign of ending, energy prices are feeding into transport, goods and eventually services. The risk, as in the 1970s, is that a supply shock becomes embedded in wages and expectations if the central bank appears hesitant.

Real interest rates tell a similar story. The yield on 10-year inflation-protected Treasuries reached its highest level since 2008 in early October. That reflects markets' belief that the Fed will keep policy tight. A clear signal of patience could undo some of that, loosening financial conditions just as inflation rises.

## Why the world is watching

The Fed's choice travels far beyond the United States. A firmer dollar and higher American rates raise borrowing costs for emerging markets, weigh on commodity prices such as gold and put pressure on other central banks to follow. The Bank of England is expected to raise rates next week partly because Washington moved first. African sovereigns refinancing dollar debt face higher costs whenever the Fed signals more to come.

For borrowers everywhere, the lesson of the past year is the same: the era of near-zero rates is not returning soon. Plans that depended on cheap refinancing need to be revisited, and the cost of time, the theme of my analysis The Price of Time, is now a permanent line in every budget.

## What to listen for

The statement and the chair's press conference will be parsed for three things: how the committee describes the inflationary effect of energy prices, whether it characterises the September move as the start of a sequence or a one-off adjustment, and how much weight it gives to the recent tightening in financial markets. A hold accompanied by firm language about December would keep the dollar strong and real yields high. A hold accompanied by caution would tell markets that the Fed is prepared to tolerate inflation above target for longer.

Either way, the real decision has been postponed rather than avoided. By the time the Fed meets in December, it will have one more inflation report, a clearer picture of the oil market and less room for ambiguity.
