Markets have a habit of treating every jump in the oil price as temporary. The futures curve slopes down, forecasters pencil in a return to normal and governments write budgets that assume the worst will pass. In 2026 that habit is being tested to destruction.

On 28 October Brent traded at about $115 a barrel. Rystad Energy reported that it had settled at $117 the previous Friday, up more than 6 per cent in a day, after Houthi strikes on Saudi infrastructure and continued disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. At the start of the month it was near $105. Trackers of shipping through the strait describe it as closed to normal commercial traffic, with only a handful of transits on some days against a usual flow of dozens, although data from Kpler showed that a substantial volume of crude was still getting through in early October. The picture is not one of a complete stoppage. It is something more corrosive: a chronic disruption, with each attack adding to insurance premiums, freight costs and the risk priced into every barrel.

## From spike to baseline

The distinction matters because policy responds differently to the two. A spike can be looked through. A new baseline cannot. The evidence that energy is feeding into the wider economy is already visible. In the euro area, inflation jumped to 3.8 per cent in September from 3.2 per cent in August, with energy prices up 18.8 per cent on a year earlier, according to Eurostat. In Britain, motor fuels were the largest contributor to the rise in inflation to 3.5 per cent. The International Monetary Fund now expects global headline inflation of 4.7 per cent this year.

> A central bank can ignore one expensive month. It cannot ignore a year in which energy keeps setting the price of everything else.

That is why the Bank of England is expected to raise rates next week, why the European Central Bank is preparing markets for a possible move in December and why the Federal Reserve has already tightened once. Each of them faces the dilemma of an energy shock: higher rates cannot produce more oil, but failing to act risks letting inflation spread into wages and expectations.

## Budgets written for a different world

Governments are exposed in a more direct way. Britain's Autumn Budget, delivered on the same day Brent was quoted at $115, included support for household energy bills and an extension of the fuel duty cut, both of which become more expensive the longer prices stay high. Suppliers have warned that bills could rise by up to a quarter in January.

For energy importers in the developing world, the arithmetic is harsher. The IMF expects growth in emerging and developing economies that are most exposed to oil and gas costs to slow to 3.7 per cent this year from 4.5 per cent in 2025. Every dollar on the barrel widens trade deficits, weakens currencies and raises the cost of servicing dollar debts. For a continent that already pays a premium to borrow, an oil shock is a debt shock by another name.

## Nigeria's paper windfall

Exporters should be winners, and on paper they are. Nigeria wrote its 2026 budget around an oil price far below today's level; analysts cited by local media put the original benchmark at about $65 a barrel, later raised by the Senate. With Brent above $110, the country is earning far more per barrel than planned.

The windfall is smaller than it looks. Production remains the constraint: estimates put August output of crude and condensate at about 1.68m barrels a day, against a budget assumption of 1.84m, a gap one analyst values at about $21m a day in forgone revenue. Higher crude prices also push up the cost of fuel at home. And the history of oil booms in Nigeria, as in many producers, is that windfalls are spent faster than they arrive. The opportunity, if the money is real, is to rebuild reserves and reduce debt rather than to finance commitments that will outlast the price.

## What investors should watch

The oil market now hinges on politics rather than geology. Talks over conditions in the strait have repeatedly stalled, and analysts disagree on how far prices could go in a full shutdown, with some arguing that $150 is plausible. The more useful question for investors is not where the price peaks but how long it stays high. A year at $110 changes inflation, interest rates and fiscal plans in a way that a month at $130 does not.

The world spent fifteen years with cheap money and, for long stretches, cheap energy. Both have now gone at the same time. The policymakers who adjust fastest to that reality, rather than waiting for the old one to return, will make the fewest mistakes.
