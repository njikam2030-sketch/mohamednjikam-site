Every textbook explanation of gold says it should be soaring. There is a war in the Gulf, the Strait of Hormuz is largely shut to normal shipping, oil is above $110 a barrel and inflation is rising across the rich world. Gold is the traditional refuge from exactly these fears.

Instead it is falling. The metal traded at about $4,024 an ounce on 27 October, according to Fortune, and slipped again the next day. Its record, set in late January, was above $5,400. Even this month it has given ground, dropping several per cent from its early-October levels.

The explanation is not that investors have stopped worrying. It is that the price of money has risen faster than their fears.

## The opportunity cost of holding a rock

Gold pays no interest. Its appeal therefore depends on what an investor gives up to hold it, and the clearest measure of that is the real yield on inflation-protected government bonds. When real yields are low or negative, holding gold costs little. When they rise, the cost of owning a metal that earns nothing goes up.

That is what has happened this autumn. The yield on 10-year US Treasury inflation-protected securities closed at 2.95 per cent on 5 October, its highest close since November 2008 and some 40 basis points above where it stood in early September. Over the following weeks, gold fell sharply. A dollar near its strongest level in a year and a half added to the pressure, since gold priced in dollars becomes more expensive for everyone else.

> Gold is not a bet on fear. It is a bet against the return on safe money, and that return has just risen to its highest level in nearly two decades.

## Inflation that strengthens central banks

There is a paradox here. Rising inflation should help gold. But this inflation is pushing central banks to tighten. The Federal Reserve raised rates in September and most of its policymakers expect to go further; the Bank of England is expected to follow next week. Each time an oil spike raises inflation fears, markets price more tightening, real yields rise and gold falls. One report on 28 October described the metal slipping precisely because an attack on a Saudi refinery revived fears of further rate rises.

Other forces have amplified the move. Losses in technology and AI-related shares have forced some funds to raise cash by selling what they could, including gold. Speculators who had built large positions during the January rally have cut them. Holdings in physically backed funds, by contrast, have remained high, which suggests that long-term investors have not abandoned the metal.

## What it tells us

The lesson extends beyond gold. For fifteen years, near-zero real interest rates lifted the price of everything that pays little or nothing today in exchange for value later: gold, long-dated bonds, technology stocks with distant profits. When real yields rise, all of these are repriced. That is the theme I explored in The Price of Time: once money is expensive again, waiting has a cost, and assets that ask investors to wait must offer more.

For central banks in emerging markets, which have been large buyers of gold in recent years as a hedge against sanctions and dollar dependence, the fall is a reminder that gold is a volatile reserve asset. For African producers, from Ghana to Mali, it means lower export revenues at a time when oil import bills are rising.

## Where next

Gold's direction now depends more on central banks than on the war. If inflation proves persistent and rates keep rising, real yields could stay high and gold could remain under pressure. If growth weakens enough for central banks to stop, or if the conflict escalates into something markets cannot price, the metal could recover quickly.

The price of gold has always been a story about fear. In October 2026 it is also a story about interest rates, and for now the interest rates are winning.
