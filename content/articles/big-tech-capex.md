There are few precedents for what the largest American technology companies are spending this year. Trackers that add up the guidance given by Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta put their combined capital expenditure for 2026 at roughly $725bn to $735bn, up by about four-fifths on the roughly $410bn they spent in 2025. Most of it is going into data centres, chips and the power to run them.

The individual figures are striking. Alphabet has guided to capital spending of $195bn to $205bn this year and has said it will rise again in 2027. Meta has raised its range more than once; one tracker records a guide of $135bn to $145bn after its third-quarter results. Microsoft's figure, around $175bn including finance leases, is lower than earlier estimates largely because of a change in lease accounting rather than a cut in ambition. The exact numbers move with each earnings call. The order of magnitude does not.

## From cash to credit

For most of the past decade these companies financed their investments from their own cash flows, with plenty left over for buybacks and dividends. At this scale, that is no longer true. As I described in Borrowing for Intelligence, the five largest hyperscalers issued about $230bn of bonds in the first nine months of the year, according to research from the Bank of Korea, more than double their total for 2025. Some analysts estimate that Meta's capital spending now exceeds its operating cash flow.

That changes who bears the risk. When an investment is paid for with retained earnings, a disappointment hurts shareholders. When it is paid for with debt, it reaches bondholders, banks, insurers and pension funds. And when it is paid for through long-term contracts with AI laboratories, as Anthropic's prospectus revealed with its $498bn of computing commitments, the risk is spread along a chain in which each company is counting on the next to keep growing.

> The question is not whether AI is real. It is whether the revenue arrives fast enough to pay for the factories before the machines inside them are obsolete.

## The depreciation problem

The economics depend on how long the assets last. Buildings, grid connections and cooling systems may serve for decades. The chips inside them lose their competitive edge within a few years as new generations arrive. Companies depreciate these assets over several years, which spreads the cost and supports reported profits during the build-out. If the useful life proves shorter than assumed, profits will have been overstated and future write-downs will follow.

Investors are beginning to ask these questions more pointedly. Some credit markets are already demanding wider spreads on debt tied to specific data centre projects and smaller developers, a sign of discipline that PIMCO and others have welcomed. The largest companies still borrow on excellent terms, because their core businesses generate enormous cash. The risk is concentrated at the edges: in specialised developers, in private credit funds that have lent to them, and in regions that have bet heavily on hosting capacity.

## What it means for everyone else

The spending has consequences far beyond Silicon Valley. It has become a meaningful source of demand in the American economy and a support for global growth, as the IMF notes. It competes with governments for long-term savings at a time when public debt is high, adding to upward pressure on bond yields. And it concentrates both stock market value and corporate borrowing in a handful of names, so that a setback at one company would travel through equity and credit markets at once.

For countries outside the supply chain, including most of Africa, the build-out is both an opportunity and a warning. The opportunity lies in hosting data centres where energy is abundant and in supplying the minerals that chips and grids require. The warning is that importing AI capacity financed in dollars, on assets that depreciate fast, would repeat old mistakes.

## The test ahead

The coming quarters will show whether revenue from AI services is growing fast enough to justify the spending. Anthropic's filing suggests that demand is real and accelerating. The prices charged for intelligence are falling, however, as competition intensifies, and the history of every infrastructure boom, from railways to fibre optics, is that the builders do not always capture the returns.

Seven hundred billion dollars is a vote of extraordinary confidence. It is also a bet placed largely with borrowed money and long contracts. The investors who do best will be those who keep asking, for every dollar spent, how long the asset will last and who will still be paying when it is gone.
