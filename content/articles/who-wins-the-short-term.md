Over the past months I have watched more than 500 videos on YouTube: interviews, conference talks and shareholder meetings with the people who run companies and manage other people's money. Chief executives of industrial groups and technology companies. Founders of private equity firms. Heads of asset managers and sovereign funds. Central bankers. Many were recorded in boom years, others in the middle of a crisis.

They disagreed about a great deal: interest rates, artificial intelligence, China, regulation, the right price for almost anything. On one point they were remarkably consistent. The best of them think in years and decades, not in days and quarters. They invest for the long term, they build for the long term, and they judge themselves over the long term.

Warren Buffett put it most bluntly in his 1988 letter to shareholders: "our favourite holding period is forever." Jeff Bezos built Amazon's first letter to shareholders, in 1997, around a single phrase: "it's all about the long term." These are not slogans. They are a description of how wealth is actually created, and the numbers support them.

But listening to those 500 leaders also left me with a question that I rarely hear asked. If the people who build lasting value all think long term, what is the short term for? And who, exactly, wins it?

## The arithmetic of the short term

Start with a simple observation. Every time someone sells a share, a bond or a currency, someone else buys it. In the short run, the price moves up or down, and one side ends up better off than the other. Before costs, the gains of the winners equal the losses of the losers.

The economist William Sharpe set this out in 1991 in a short paper called "The Arithmetic of Active Management". Taken together, investors own the market. Before costs, the average active investor must therefore earn the market return, because collectively they are the market. After costs, which active trading always incurs, the average active investor must earn less. It is not a theory about skill. It is arithmetic.

<figure><img src="/images/charts/who-wins-short-term.svg" alt="Who wins in the short term? Investor A sells to Investor B through an intermediary paid on every trade." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 1. Before costs, short-term trading is a contest in which one side's gain is the other's loss. After costs, the group loses, and the intermediary is paid either way.</figcaption></figure>

This is the first answer to the question. In the short term, the reliable winner is not the trader who guesses right. It is whoever is paid on every transaction, whichever way the market moves: the broker's commission, the market maker's spread, the platform's fee, the fund's charges. When markets rise, they are paid. When markets fall, they are paid. When interest rates jump, volumes rise and they are paid more.

> In the short term, everyone is trying not to be the loser. The intermediary does not need to try.

## What the evidence shows

If the arithmetic is right, those who trade the most should do worst. They do.

In a study that has become a classic, Brad Barber and Terrance Odean examined the accounts of tens of thousands of households at a large US discount broker in the 1990s. The households that traded most earned 11.4 per cent a year. The market returned 17.9 per cent. The average household turned over 75 per cent of its portfolio each year; the most active, more than 250 per cent. The title of their paper summed it up: "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth".

Three decades later, the pattern has not changed. Morningstar's 2026 "Mind the Gap" study found that over the ten years to 2025, the average dollar invested in US funds and ETFs earned 8.7 per cent a year, while the funds themselves returned 9.9 per cent. The 1.2-point gap, worth an estimated $3.8tn of forgone wealth, came not from the funds but from investors' own timing: buying after prices had risen, selling after they had fallen. The gap was largest in the most volatile funds.

Professionals do not escape the arithmetic. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecards show that over 15 years, more than 90 per cent of actively managed US large-cap funds have lagged the S&P 500. And in 2008 Buffett wagered $1m that a simple S&P 500 index fund would beat a selection of hedge funds over ten years. By the end of 2017, the index fund had compounded at about 7.1 per cent a year; the hedge fund selection at about 2.2 per cent.

## Measuring the losses, and who collects them

The gap is not spread evenly. Morningstar found that investors in the least volatile funds lost only about 0.4 percentage points a year to bad timing, while those in the most volatile funds lost more than 2 points. The starkest example is recent: investors in US spot bitcoin ETFs trailed the funds they owned by more than 14 percentage points a year, losing 5.8 per cent a year since January 2024 while the funds themselves returned 8.5 per cent. The more exciting the product, the more people trade it, and the more they lose.

Costs compound in the same way as returns. Take $100,000 invested for 30 years at 7 per cent a year: it grows to about $761,000. Reduce the return by one percentage point a year, the typical cost of frequent trading, spreads and active fees, and it grows to about $574,000. The difference, about $187,000 or a quarter of the final sum, does not disappear. It is collected, year after year, by the intermediaries on the other side of the transactions.

## IPOs: who wins the first day?

Initial public offerings are where short-term thinking is most visible, and where the question of who wins is easiest to answer. According to Jay Ritter's long-running data, US IPOs rose by an average of about 18 per cent on their first day of trading between 1980 and 2014. That first-day gain goes mostly to the investors who received shares at the offer price, typically large institutions with close relationships to the underwriting banks, and the banks themselves earn their fees on the full amount raised. The company and its existing shareholders, who sold below the price the market was willing to pay, effectively leave money on the table.

Those who buy after the first day face a different arithmetic. Ritter's work shows that, on average, IPOs have underperformed the market over their first three years, and on an equal-weighted basis underperformed companies of similar size by about 3.1 percentage points a year over five years. More than half have lost money over three and five years. Excitement is priced in on day one; performance has to be earned over many years.

The largest test of all is under way. On 10 November, Anthropic launched the roadshow for its Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT, offering 825m shares at $70 to $78 each. At the top of the range it would raise about $64bn, with a further $26bn of shares committed by strategic partners including Google and Nvidia, and a valuation of up to about $1.4tn. Pricing is expected around 18 November, according to press reports. As I wrote when the company filed its prospectus, the business is real and growing fast: revenue was about $16.3bn in the first half of 2026. But at the top of the range, investors would pay roughly 30 times annualised second-quarter revenue. Those who receive allocations may well enjoy a first-day gain. Whether long-term buyers do well will depend on something slower and harder: whether the company's growth over the next decade justifies the price paid in the next week.

## My own analysis: time turns risk into return

To test the long-term case directly, I used Robert Shiller's monthly data on US stocks going back to 1871, with dividends reinvested and returns adjusted for inflation. I measured every possible holding period, month by month, and asked a simple question: how often did an investor lose money after inflation?

<figure><img src="/images/charts/time-in-the-market.svg" alt="Share of holding periods with a real loss: 31% over 1 year, 19% over 5 years, 11% over 10 years, 0.06% over 20 years, 0% over 30 years." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 2. Over one year, US stocks lost money after inflation in 31 per cent of periods. Over 20 years, in 0.06 per cent. Over 30 years, never. Source: Robert Shiller data, 1871–2023; author's calculations.</figcaption></figure>

Over a single year, an investor lost money in real terms 31 per cent of the time. Over five years, 19 per cent. Over ten years, 11 per cent. Over twenty years, the share falls to 0.06 per cent: a single starting month, with a loss of about 0.2 per cent a year. Over thirty years, there was no period with a real loss at all; the worst thirty-year stretch still delivered about 1.9 per cent a year above inflation, and the median about 6.7 per cent.

The short term is dominated by noise: news, fear, interest rate surprises, other people's decisions. The long term is dominated by something else entirely: the profits, dividends and growth of the businesses being owned.

The second test concerns timing. I took $1 invested in US stocks in January 1990 and followed it to mid-2023, then asked what would have happened to an investor who missed only a handful of the best months.

<figure><img src="/images/charts/missing-best-months.svg" alt="$1 invested in 1990 became $25.1 if invested throughout, $15.3 missing the 5 best months, $10.9 missing the 10 best and $6.0 missing the 20 best." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Figure 3. Missing the ten best months out of more than 400 cut the final result by more than half. Source: Robert Shiller data, monthly averages, nominal total return; author's calculations.</figcaption></figure>

Staying invested turned $1 into about $25, roughly 10 per cent a year. Missing the five best months reduced it to about $15. Missing the ten best, to about $11. Missing the twenty best, to about $6. The best months tend to arrive close to the worst, in periods of panic, exactly when the short-term investor is most tempted to sell.

## Companies face the same choice

The same logic applies to the companies themselves. In 2017 the McKinsey Global Institute and FCLTGlobal built an index of corporate long-termism across 615 large and mid-sized US companies, based on investment, earnings quality and the habit of managing numbers to hit quarterly targets. Between 2001 and 2014, the long-term companies grew their cumulative revenue 47 per cent more than the others, their earnings 36 per cent more, and added on average about $7bn more in market value. The authors called it a first estimate, and some economists consider the evidence suggestive rather than conclusive. But it points in the same direction as everything else.

This is what the leaders I listened to understand. A private equity firm that holds a company for seven years, a sovereign fund that invests for the next generation, a family business that thinks about the grandchildren: each can afford to ignore the quarter because its returns come from what the business becomes, not from what the next buyer will pay tomorrow.

## When markets rise, fall, or rates move

Short-term thinking assumes that the direction of the market is what matters. For a long-term owner, it matters much less.

When markets rise, the short-term trader who sold too early loses to the one who bought; the long-term owner simply owns more value. When markets fall, the trader who is forced to sell crystallises the loss; the long-term owner, if not forced to sell, collects the same dividends and can buy more at lower prices. When interest rates move, the price of a bond changes every day, but an investor who holds it to maturity still receives the coupons and the principal promised at the start, provided the borrower pays.

In each case the short-term outcome depends on another participant's decision. The long-term outcome depends on cash flows.

> Short-term returns are a transfer between investors. Long-term returns are created by businesses.

## So who really benefits from short-termism?

Putting it together, short-termism benefits three groups. First, intermediaries paid per transaction, whose income rises with activity and volatility rather than with performance. Second, the minority with a genuine edge in speed, information or analysis, whose gains come largely from less informed participants. Third, and most interestingly, the patient long-term investor, who can buy from those forced to sell and sell to those desperate to buy. The short-term crowd supplies liquidity; the long-term investor uses it.

The group that consistently loses is the one in the middle: the investor who trades frequently, without an edge, reacting to headlines.

## A message to the next generation

This is the part that matters most to me. The same arithmetic applies to careers, skills and lives. Young people today are surrounded by short-term signals: daily price charts, viral trading apps, overnight success stories. Each invites them to treat life as a series of quick trades.

The leaders I listened to built something different: knowledge that compounds, relationships that compound, reputations that compound. None of them got rich, or wise, in a quarter.

The question I would put to any young person is the one this article began with. Before you chase a short-term gain, ask who is on the other side of the trade, and who is paid for it whatever happens. If you cannot answer, the short term is not working for you. Time is.
