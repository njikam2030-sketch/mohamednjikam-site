The European Central Bank meets on Thursday with a problem it hoped it had left behind. Euro area inflation rose to 3.8 per cent in September, from 3.2 per cent in August and 2.2 per cent a year earlier, according to Eurostat. Energy prices were up 18.8 per cent on the year. Services inflation, the measure the ECB watches most closely for signs of domestic pressure, stood at 3.2 per cent.

Almost nobody expects the bank to move this week. Its deposit rate has been 2.5 per cent since September, a Reuters poll found 70 of 73 economists expecting no change, and market pricing put the chance of an October move at only a few per cent. But the same poll found 64 economists expecting a quarter-point rise in December. The meeting matters less for what the ECB does than for what it says about the next one.

## Why wait?

The case for patience is real. The euro area is growing slowly: the International Monetary Fund expects output to expand by just 1.1 per cent this year. Most of the rise in inflation comes from energy, which interest rates cannot make cheaper. And unlike the Federal Reserve or the Bank of England, the ECB sets policy for twenty economies with very different exposures to the oil shock and very different fiscal positions. A move that suits Germany may hurt Italy; a pause that suits Spain may look complacent in the Baltic states, where inflation is well above the average.

There is also a question of credibility. The ECB raised rates aggressively in 2022 and 2023, cut them as inflation fell, and would now be reversing course. Central banks dislike being seen to zigzag, and a well-signalled move in December, backed by fresh staff forecasts, is easier to defend than a surprise in October.

> The ECB can afford to wait one meeting. It cannot afford to let markets believe it will wait indefinitely.

## Why December looks likely

The case for tightening is growing stronger by the week. With Brent above $110 a barrel and the disruption in the Strait of Hormuz showing little sign of ending, energy is no longer a temporary shock. Higher fuel and electricity prices feed into transport, food and services with a lag, and wage negotiations across the euro area are beginning to reflect the higher cost of living.

The ECB also faces pressure from abroad. The Federal Reserve raised rates in September, and the Bank of England is expected to follow next week. If the euro area holds while others tighten, the euro could weaken, adding to imported inflation at precisely the wrong moment.

## Why it matters in Africa

The decision will be watched closely in fourteen African countries whose currencies are pegged to the euro through the CFA franc. When the ECB tightens, the central banks of the West and Central African monetary unions face pressure to follow, to protect their reserves and the peg, whatever the state of their own economies. Central Africa's BEAC cut its policy rate in June, the opposite direction to Frankfurt. A December hike in Frankfurt would narrow that room for manoeuvre and put the trade-offs of the monetary union back at the centre of the debate.

## What to listen for

On Thursday, the important signals will be in the language rather than the decision. Investors will want to hear how the ECB describes the persistence of energy prices, whether it sees signs of second-round effects in wages and services, and how firmly it ties the December meeting to the new staff projections. A president who stresses vigilance and data dependence is preparing the ground for a move. One who stresses the weakness of growth is buying time.

The ECB's choice is not really between acting and waiting. It is between telling markets clearly what would make it act, and leaving them to guess. In a year of energy shocks and nervous bond markets, clarity is worth more than a quarter point.
