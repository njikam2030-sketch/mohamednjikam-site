On Budget day Westminster speaks a curious dialect. Ministers, journalists and analysts argue about "headroom" as though it were a measure of national health. This year the Office for Budget Responsibility put it at £15bn in 2029-30, down from £23.6bn in March. The gilt market approved, yields barely moved, and the Chancellor could claim that discipline had been restored.

Discipline matters. The memory of 2022, when unfunded promises sent gilt yields soaring within days, is still fresh on every trading floor in the City. But in a week like this one it is worth making an unfashionable argument: Britain's difficulty is not a shortage of good fiscal rules. It is a shortage of growth that can be relied upon.

A fiscal rule is a promise about the future. Debt will fall as a share of output within five years; day-to-day spending will be covered by revenue. It is useful because it forces a government to show its arithmetic. But the arithmetic rests on a forecast, and the forecast rests above all on one variable, the rate at which the economy grows. When the OBR nudges growth up to 1.2 per cent for 2026 and cuts it to 1 per cent for 2027, headroom moves by billions. When productivity disappoints, headroom vanishes, and a new Chancellor must raise taxes or cut spending to stay within a rule written by the last one. Britain has rewritten its fiscal framework many times since 2010. Each version was presented as a fresh start. Each ran into the same wall.

> A rule tells you whether you are keeping your promises. It does not tell you how to make them affordable.

The debt ratio has two parts: what the state owes and what the economy produces. Nearly all political energy goes into the first. The larger prize lies in the second. A country growing at 2.5 per cent a year can carry debts that would crush one growing at 1 per cent, and over a decade the difference dwarfs any surcharge on banks or freeze in tax thresholds. The productivity slowdown since 2008 is, to my mind, the most important British economic fact of the past two decades. It explains why wages stagnated, why public services feel stretched despite higher taxes, and why every Budget now feels like a negotiation with bondholders rather than a plan for the country.

A Budget built around the denominator would make different choices. It would treat planning reform, grid connections and transport as fiscal policy, because they decide whether private investment arrives. It would hold capital spending steady through the cycle instead of cutting it first whenever headroom shrinks. It would judge tax changes by their effect on investment and work, not only by what they raise within the forecast window.

Some of this year's measures point the right way. A higher threshold for small business rates relief, a lower capital gains rate for founders on their first £10m and money for AI security all speak to investment. But they are fragments. There is still no single, credible account of how Britain will raise output per hour by the end of the decade.

It would be easy to conclude that the gilt market forbids ambition. The opposite is closer to the truth. Investors do not punish plans for growth; they punish plans that are unfunded, unclear or likely to be reversed. A 10-year yield of 5.31 per cent is a price for uncertainty as much as for inflation. A government that pursued a consistent investment strategy under stable rules, year after year, would earn a lower risk premium over time.

Fiscal discipline is necessary. It is not sufficient. The next Chancellor who wants to escape the headroom trap should spend less time designing rules and more time designing growth. The bond market will notice.
