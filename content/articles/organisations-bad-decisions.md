When a company makes a bad decision, the first instinct is to look for the person responsible. Sometimes there is one. More often the decision was taken by intelligent, well-meaning people doing precisely what the organisation rewarded them for.

The quality of a company's decisions depends less on the intelligence of its people than on the design of the organisation around them. Four mechanisms account for most of the damage.

## Incentives beat intentions

People respond to what they are measured and paid on. Reward a sales team on revenue and it will grant discounts that destroy margin. Reward a project manager for delivering on time and problems will stay hidden until the deadline. Reward a lending officer on volume and credit quality will drift. None of this requires bad intent. It requires only that people behave rationally inside the system they work in.

> Show me the incentive and I will show you the decision.

The first question after a poor decision should therefore not be who made it, but what that person was rewarded for. The answer usually explains more than any examination of their judgement.

## The truth is filtered on the way up

In any hierarchy, information is summarised as it climbs. Each layer simplifies, strips out uncertainty and, consciously or not, presents the story in a light that flatters itself. By the time a problem reaches the board it can look smaller, later and more manageable than it is. Peter Drucker observed that the most important thing in communication is hearing what isn't said. In organisations, what isn't said is usually the bad news.

Leaders who want the truth have to go and fetch it: visiting operations, talking to people several levels down, reading raw data rather than polished summaries, and making it safe to say that something is not working.

## Money already spent

Once a company has spent heavily on a project, abandoning it feels like confessing failure, so the project continues, consuming more money, because stopping would make the earlier spending look wasted. Economically, sunk costs are irrelevant; only future costs and benefits should count. Psychologically, they are the most powerful force in the room. The executive who launched the project has a reputation at stake, the team has careers at stake, and the board does not want to write off an investment it approved.

The best defence is to agree, before a project begins, on the evidence that would end it. When that evidence appears, the decision has already been taken in calmer conditions.

The pattern is familiar. A company launches an ambitious product and early sales disappoint. The team explains that customers need time and that the next version will fix the problems; the sponsoring executive agrees; the board is reassured. A year later sales are still weak, more has been spent on marketing and development, and the original business case is no longer mentioned. Eventually a new chief executive closes the product and writes off the investment. Every mechanism described here was at work, and a stopping rule agreed at launch would have saved most of the money.

## Consensus that is really conformity

Teams that work well together develop shared assumptions. That is useful until it is not. When everyone in the room thinks alike, nobody tests the premises and dissent begins to feel disloyal. The psychologist Irving Janis called it groupthink, and its symptoms are easy to recognise: an illusion of invulnerability, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship and the belief that silence means agreement. Some of the most expensive failures in corporate and financial history were approved unanimously.

None of these tendencies can be eliminated, but they can be managed. Organisations that decide well reward margin as well as revenue, quality as well as speed, and defer part of bonuses until results are proven. They make dissent a formal role, asking someone to argue the opposite case before big decisions so that disagreement carries no social cost. They set stopping rules in advance, place small bets before large ones, keep the distance between leaders and reality short, and review the reasoning behind decisions rather than only their results, since a sound decision can turn out badly through bad luck and a poor one can be rescued by good luck.

## Finance as the honest broker

The finance function has a particular responsibility. It sees the numbers across the whole organisation, it is less attached to any single project and it can ask awkward questions in neutral language. A finance team that merely reports results wastes that position. One that challenges assumptions, tracks projects against their original business cases and makes sunk costs visible can improve every major decision the company takes.

Banks and investment firms are especially exposed, because their decisions involve risks that surface only later. A loan that looks profitable today may become a loss in three years; a trading strategy that thrives in calm markets may fail in a crisis. That is why the best financial institutions invest in independent risk functions, separate those who take risks from those who measure them, and pay part of bonuses in deferred form. These are not bureaucratic burdens. They are designs that counter the natural tendency of capable people to underestimate the risks they are paid to take.

Smart organisations make bad decisions when their structures reward the wrong things, bury the truth and punish disagreement. The remedy is not to hire cleverer people. It is to build organisations in which ordinary people, acting rationally, are led towards good decisions.
