Within minutes of John Healey sitting down on Wednesday, one fund manager had a name for it: "the boring Budget that bond markets demanded". It was meant as praise, and in the circumstances it deserves to be taken as such.

Three weeks earlier, the 30-year gilt had touched about 6.05 per cent, its highest level since 1998, and the 10-year yield had climbed to levels last seen in 2007. A Chancellor speaking in that climate addresses two audiences at once: the MPs in front of him and the investors who will decide, before he has finished, whether his numbers are believable. Healey chose the second. The choice says more about the state of the British economy than anything in the Red Book.

## A thinner cushion

The Office for Budget Responsibility now puts the government's headroom against its fiscal rules at £15bn in 2029-30, down from £23.6bn in March, rising to £22bn the following year. Healey blamed the conflict in the Middle East, which has pushed up borrowing across the G7, and defended spending part of the margin. "That is what a buffer is for," he told the Commons. "Use it when you need it, rebuild it when you can."

It is a good line and a sound principle. It is also a reminder of how little room there is. The OBR expects public debt at 83.2 per cent of GDP this year, edging up to 83.8 per cent in 2028-29 before easing to 81.7 per cent by 2032. At those levels, a few tenths of a point on gilt yields, or a single disappointing year of growth, can consume most of £15bn. Everyone on a gilt desk knows it.

## What the market heard

Fund managers had said before the speech that anything below £10bn would disappoint; a Reuters poll pointed to between £10bn and £12bn. Healey found £15bn. Gilts rallied in the morning and then barely moved while he spoke, the market's way of saying the news was already in the price. By the afternoon, they were outperforming US Treasuries by about five basis points, and the 10-year traded at 5.31 per cent, some 20 basis points below this month's peak.

The approval was earned by omission. There were no large unfunded giveaways. Healey inherited one of the tightest fiscal plans in the G10 and departed from it only at the margins. Borrowing is forecast to fall from 4.2 per cent of GDP this year to 3.4 per cent next, and to 1.8 per cent between 2030 and 2032.

> In October 2026, the most valuable thing a Chancellor could offer was not a tax cut. It was predictability.

That connects to an argument I made a fortnight ago in The Price of Time. When money is expensive, credibility acquires a price that can be measured. Every basis point the gilt market declines to add is a saving for the Treasury, for mortgage holders and for every company that borrows off the gilt curve.

## The banks escape lightly

The most political measure was a surcharge on bank profits. Reports before the Budget had suggested 8 per cent. It arrived at 3 per cent, applying from January 2027 and expiring 15 months later. Bank shares rose in the morning as the leaks proved accurate.

The logic is easy to follow. The Treasury needed revenue to fund support for households facing higher energy bills, and lenders have done well out of wider interest margins. A modest, temporary levy raises money without alarming the sector that finances everyone else. But a 15-month tax also reveals an assumption: that the pressure on household budgets will pass. If rates stay higher for longer, as the Federal Reserve's own projections imply, that assumption looks generous.

The growth forecasts carry a similar warning. The OBR raised its estimate for 2026 from 1.1 to 1.2 per cent, and Healey was quick to cite it. In the same document, the forecast for 2027 was cut from 1.6 to 1 per cent. The arithmetic of the fiscal rules rests on growth. A weaker 2027 means lower receipts and higher spending, and if the slowdown is deeper than expected, the £15bn will not last long.

## The reform nobody will thank him for

Buried among the short-term measures was a decision whose effects will be felt for a generation. The triple lock on the state pension will end from 2030, replaced by a "double lock", with savings the government puts at about £50bn by 2050. It does nothing for the next two years and a great deal for the debt path of the 2040s. It is the kind of reform bond markets reward and voters rarely applaud, and it may be the most consequential line in the speech precisely because it was the least glamorous.

What comes next will test the calm. Analysts now regard the Debt Management Office's issuance plan as the more important event of the autumn: more Treasury bills and fewer long-dated gilts would support the long end, while heavier long issuance could undo the day's composure in a session. The Bank of England meets on 5 November with inflation at 3.5 per cent and markets pricing a rise in Bank Rate to 4 per cent as close to certain. Suppliers have warned that energy bills could rise by up to a quarter in January, and if they do, the pressure to spend will return before the ink is dry.

This was not a Budget designed to be loved. It was designed to be believed, and with Britain paying more than 5 per cent to borrow for ten years, that was the right priority. Healey understood who holds the pen in British fiscal policy today: not the Treasury alone, but the buyers of its debt.

The danger is that boredom becomes a strategy rather than a starting point. Credibility buys time; it does not buy growth. The next Budget will have to do what this one carefully avoided, and explain how Britain intends to grow its way out of an 83 per cent debt ratio rather than merely manage it.
