When M-Pesa launched in Kenya in 2007, few people outside the continent grasped what was happening. Within a few years millions who had never held a bank account were sending money, paying bills and receiving wages on their phones. Africa had skipped the branch network altogether.

That leap was about payments. The next one is about capital, and it matters more.

Mobile money solved the problem of moving money cheaply and safely. It brought people into the financial system and made small transactions practical, and in many countries mobile wallets now hold substantial balances. But a wallet is not an investment. Most of the money sitting in mobile accounts earns little or nothing for its owners, even when the operators earn interest on the balances they place with banks. Meanwhile African governments and companies borrow abroad, in dollars, at high rates, because local long-term capital is scarce.

> Africa has savings. What it lacks are the channels that turn those savings into long-term local investment.

## The price of borrowing abroad

The cost of the present arrangement is plain in the bond market. In February, Côte d'Ivoire raised $1.3bn over 15 years at an effective rate of about 5.39 per cent after hedging into euros, a strong result. Others pay far more: DR Congo's debut 10-year bond cost 9.5 per cent, and Kenya refinanced at 10.4 per cent a bond first issued at 6.9 per cent.

Sub-Saharan sovereigns raised about $9.3bn in eurobonds between January and September, more than in the whole of 2025. Their repayments, according to the World Bank, rise from about $3.5bn this year to $8.6bn in 2028. Every dollar carries currency risk. When the local currency weakens, the burden grows even if nothing else changes.

Deep local capital markets reduce that vulnerability. Governments and companies able to borrow in their own currency, from their own citizens and institutions, are less exposed to swings in global rates and exchange rates. Savers earn a return and capital stays at home. Many of the building blocks already exist: government securities markets, regional exchanges such as the BRVM in west Africa, growing pension funds and asset managers. What is missing is scale and access. Most ordinary savers cannot easily buy a government bond or a diversified fund.

## The wallet as a gateway

This is where mobile money becomes more than a payments system. The same infrastructure that lets someone send money to a relative could let them buy a small slice of a government bond, contribute to a pension or invest in a regulated fund. Several countries have already experimented with bonds and savings products distributed by phone. Millions of small savers, each putting aside modest sums regularly, can add up to a meaningful pool of local long-term capital.

It will not happen on its own. Savers must be confident their money is safe, which demands strong regulation, clear disclosure and vigorous action against fraud. Products must be simple to understand and easy to exit, because complexity is the enemy of inclusion. Nobody will lend to their own government for ten years if inflation erodes their savings, so credible central banks and sound public finances come first. Wallets, banks, exchanges and custodians must connect smoothly, ideally across borders within the regional unions. And savers need to understand the difference between a deposit, a bond and a share.

## Why it matters for growth

Infrastructure, housing, energy and manufacturing all need long-term finance, and much of it now comes from abroad, in foreign currency, at a high price. If even a fraction of the savings flowing through African phones could be channelled into local investment, the cost of capital would fall and the continent would depend less on the mood of global markets. That is what the price of time means for Africa: capital that stays at home, in local currency, does not have to be repaid at the mercy of the dollar.

Size is the obstacle many markets face alone. A single country's bond or equity market may lack enough issuers and investors to be liquid, and regional integration changes that. The West African Economic and Monetary Union already shares a currency, a central bank and a regional stock exchange. Central Africa has its own monetary union and is working to integrate its financial markets, which matters directly for Cameroon. The African Continental Free Trade Area aims to lower barriers to trade across the continent, and financial integration is the natural next step. For savers, regional markets mean more choice and better diversification; for issuers, a larger pool of investors in a shared currency. Mobile platforms operating across borders within these unions could become the distribution channel that opens regional markets to ordinary people.

Policymakers have the levers. They can issue more local-currency debt in small denominations, sold through mobile channels where regulation allows; strengthen pension systems so that more long-term savings are invested at home; regulate digital investment products firmly and pursue fraud; encourage local listings so that citizens can share in the success of growing companies; and measure progress honestly, counting not only accounts opened but long-term capital mobilised.

Africa's first financial leap connected people to payments. The second must connect their savings to their own economies. It is harder, because it depends on trust, stability and good regulation rather than technology alone. The reward is a continent that finances more of its own future.
