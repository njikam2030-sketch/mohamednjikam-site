Every boom has a story, and every story comes with a spreadsheet that makes it look inevitable. In 2026 the story is artificial intelligence and the numbers are extraordinary. The five largest American hyperscalers issued about $230bn of bonds in the first nine months of the year, according to research from the Bank of Korea. IPO proceeds have reached record levels. In boardrooms everywhere the same question is asked: what is our AI strategy, and why are we not spending more on it?

Anyone who has sat in the finance director's chair knows how this moment feels from the inside. The pressure to say yes is enormous, and it seldom looks like pressure. It looks like optimism, momentum and the fear of being left behind.

In a boom, money is easy to raise, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. When investors are eager, the cost of capital appears low, and projects that would never survive a disciplined review suddenly seem reasonable. The risk is not that the technology is false. It is that the price paid for it assumes a future that is far from assured. The CFO's first duty is to separate the story from the cash flows. What revenue will the investment generate, when, and on what assumptions? What happens if adoption is slower, prices fall faster or the assets are obsolete in three years rather than seven? Those questions are not hostile to innovation. They are the condition for financing it sustainably.

> In a boom, the most valuable word in the finance function is neither yes nor no. It is "assuming".

One risk deserves particular attention: the mismatch between long-dated financing and short-lived assets. Chips and servers can lose much of their value within a few years, while the bonds that paid for them run for a decade or more. If the economics of the assets change, the debt remains. It is a classic treasury problem, and it does not disappear because the technology is new.

The hardest moment to say no is the moment itself. The best protection is to agree the rules before the pressure arrives: the maximum leverage the company will accept, the payback period for new investment, the share of capital that can go to a single theme. Once those limits are written down and approved by the board, the CFO is no longer fighting the chief executive's ambition, but defending a decision the company has already made. It also helps to change the vocabulary. Rather than rejecting a project, finance can ask for a staged plan: a modest first phase with clear milestones, followed by larger commitments only when the evidence arrives. That protects the downside without killing the opportunity.

Behind all this sits a quality worth more than any technical skill, which is humility. Nobody knows how the AI cycle will end. The most dangerous executives are those who are certain, in either direction. The best finance teams hold two ideas at once: that this technology is transformative, and that many of the investments made in its name will fail.

Booms are not won by those who spend the most, but by those still standing when the cycle turns. The CFO's job is to make sure the company is among them, even if that sometimes means saying no to a very good story.
