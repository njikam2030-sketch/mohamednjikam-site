China's economy grew by 4.5 per cent in the third quarter from a year earlier, according to the National Bureau of Statistics, slightly faster than the 4.3 per cent recorded in the second quarter and a touch above the consensus forecast of 4.4 per cent. Growth over the first nine months was 4.6 per cent, comfortably inside Beijing's target range of 4.5 to 5 per cent for the year. On a quarterly basis, output rose 1.2 per cent.

For a government that has spent two years fighting a property slump and deflationary pressure, those are respectable numbers. They will reassure officials that the target is within reach. They should not reassure anyone about the shape of the recovery.

## The supply side carries the load

The strength is concentrated in factories. Industrial output rose 5.5 per cent in September from a year earlier, accelerating from 5.2 per cent in August, and industrial profits jumped 14.4 per cent, the fastest pace in three months, led by miners and chipmakers. Exports have surged, helped by global demand for electronics and equipment linked to the AI build-out.

The weakness is at home. Retail sales grew by just 1.1 per cent in September. That beat forecasts of around 0.5 per cent and improved on August's 0.4 per cent, but it is a very low rate of growth for an economy of China's size and ambition. Fixed-asset investment in urban areas fell 7.2 per cent in the first nine months of the year, and real estate development investment dropped 20.6 per cent, a deeper decline than in the first eight months.

> China is growing the way it has grown for decades: by producing more than its own households are willing to buy.

## Why households hold back

The reasons are well known. Property has long been the main store of wealth for Chinese families, and falling prices have eroded their sense of security. Youth unemployment remains a concern, and the social safety net is thin enough that households save heavily against illness and old age. Government stimulus has favoured infrastructure and industry over direct support for consumption.

The result is an economy that depends on external demand to absorb its surplus production. That works while the rest of the world is buying. It becomes a source of friction when trading partners worry about a flood of cheap Chinese goods, and a source of vulnerability if global demand slows.

## What it means for the rest of the world

For the West, China's pattern of growth means continued competition in manufacturing, from electric vehicles to solar panels, and continued pressure on trade policy. For commodity exporters, including many African economies, it means demand for metals tied to industry and technology rather than to construction, a shift visible in the strength of mining profits.

For Africa there is a further dimension. China remains one of the continent's most important trading partners and creditors. A Chinese economy that grows through exports and industry rather than domestic consumption will keep seeking markets and resources abroad, but may be more cautious about new lending as it manages problems at home.

## The test ahead

The International Monetary Fund expects Chinese growth to slow to 4 per cent next year. Whether it does so gently will depend less on factories than on households. A recovery that rests on industry and exports can meet a growth target; it cannot by itself deliver the rebalancing towards consumption that Beijing has promised for more than a decade.

The third-quarter figures show that the Chinese economy is stable. They do not yet show that it is changing. Investors should welcome the first and keep waiting for the second.
