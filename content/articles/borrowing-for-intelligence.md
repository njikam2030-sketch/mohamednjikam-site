For most of the past decade, the largest technology companies had the opposite of a debt problem. They generated so much cash that the main question for their finance departments was how to give it back. Buybacks, dividends and acquisitions absorbed the surplus.

That era is closing. In 2026 the builders of artificial intelligence have become some of the largest issuers of corporate debt in the world, and the shift remains underappreciated outside credit markets.

Estimates vary because definitions do. Research from the Bank of Korea counts about $230bn of bonds issued by the five largest US hyperscalers in the first nine months of the year, more than double their total for the whole of 2025. Broader tallies, which include utilities, data centre developers and chipmakers, put AI-related borrowing this year somewhere between $450bn and well over $500bn. On the spending side, Goldman Sachs projects hyperscaler capital expenditure of $600bn to $820bn in 2026, possibly above $1tn in 2027; S&P Global Ratings expects roughly $750bn from the five largest companies. Pick whichever number you prefer. The order of magnitude is the story: a new industrial base is being built, and an increasing share of it is being built on credit.

## Why the rich borrow

It seems odd that companies with such enormous cash flows should need to borrow at all. The simplest explanation is that the spending has outgrown even their cash generation. Data centres, chips and power contracts at this pace absorb more than operating cash can cover without cutting shareholder returns, and no board wants to explain that to investors.

Debt is also cheaper than equity for highly rated companies, even at today's rates. If an executive believes a data centre will earn more than it costs to finance, issuing bonds is rational. And capital spending is depreciated over years, which flatters near-term profits while the build-out continues.

None of this is reckless. But it changes who carries the risk. When an investment is funded from retained earnings, a disappointment hurts shareholders. When it is funded with debt, it reaches bondholders, banks, insurers and pension funds.

## The mismatch

The part of the story that deserves the most attention is the gap between the life of the debt and the life of what it buys.

> Long-dated debt is being used to finance assets whose economic life may be short.

A 20- or 30-year bond is a promise that the borrower will still be paying in the 2040s and 2050s. A data centre's buildings and grid connections may well last that long. The chips inside them will not. Each generation of AI hardware has made its predecessor less competitive within a few years. If revenue from AI services grows fast enough, the mismatch does not matter, because the cash flows pay for replacement. If revenue lags the spending, companies face a choice between cutting investment and borrowing more. Some credit investors are already demanding wider spreads on project-specific debt and on smaller data centre developers, which is the market doing exactly what it should.

In Intelligence on Tap I argued that AI is becoming a utility, priced and consumed like electricity. The financing confirms the comparison, up to a point. Utilities have always been funded with debt because their assets are long-lived and their revenues predictable. AI revenues are not yet predictable. Demand is clearly growing; who will capture the margins is not. It may be the chip designers, the cloud providers, the model builders or the companies that use AI to sell something else. Financing an industry with utility-style debt before it has utility-style revenues is a wager. It may prove a good one. It is a wager all the same.

## What it does to markets

The consequences spread well beyond technology. Large AI issues compete with government bonds for the same long-term investors at a time when governments are borrowing heavily themselves, which adds upward pressure on long-term yields. A handful of companies now account for a large share of both equity market value and new corporate issuance, so a setback at one of them would travel through both markets at once. And the build-out is a test of credit discipline: wider spreads on weaker borrowers are healthy, while spreads that stayed tight because investors assumed the biggest names could never disappoint would be a warning.

It helps to think in scenarios rather than predictions. In the benign case, enterprise adoption arrives at scale, pricing holds and data centre cash flows repay the debt comfortably; today's borrowing will look like the early financing of the railways or electrification, expensive but justified. In the grim case, slower adoption, falling prices and higher rates leave too much capacity chasing too little revenue, asset values fall and the most indebted companies cut investment sharply. The technology survives; many of its financiers do not.

The likeliest outcome lies between the two. Demand grows, but competition pushes prices down faster than expected. The largest companies stay profitable while returns on new capacity fall, and specialised developers and smaller cloud providers struggle to refinance. That is the world in which credit analysis earns its keep, because it separates borrowers who can carry their debt from those who cannot. A lender would want to know how the useful life of the assets compares with the maturity of the bonds, how much revenue is contracted and for how long, what happens to cash flow if AI prices halve, and how much spending could be paused without tripping a covenant.

## A lesson for Africa

African governments and companies should watch closely. The temptation will be to import AI capacity quickly, financed in dollars, for fear of being left behind. That would repeat an old mistake: borrowing in foreign currency to buy assets that lose value fast.

The wiser course is to finance the durable layers of the AI economy, the generation and transmission of power, fibre, data centre shells and, above all, skills. These keep their value across hardware cycles. Chips can be leased or bought as a service. Power stations and trained engineers cannot be rented from abroad at the moment they are needed.

Artificial intelligence is being built on borrowed money. That does not make it a bubble. It makes it a credit story, with all the discipline that implies, and the investors who do best will not be those who believe most fervently in the technology. They will be the ones who ask, for every dollar lent, how long the asset will last and who will still be paying when it is gone.
