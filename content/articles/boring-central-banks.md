In November the Bank of England is expected to raise Bank Rate from 3.75 to 4 per cent. On 28 October, market pricing put the probability at about 97 per cent. The criticism has already begun. Why tighten when the inflation comes from energy, when growth is feeble and households are still recovering from the last squeeze?

It is a fair question. The Bank should go ahead anyway, and it should stop sounding apologetic about it.

Textbook economics says central banks should "look through" supply shocks. If prices rise because energy is scarce, higher interest rates will not produce more oil or gas; they will only slow the rest of the economy. That advice holds for a one-off shock that leaves expectations undisturbed. It becomes dangerous when inflation is already above target and has been for years. UK inflation rose to 3.5 per cent in September, from 3.1 per cent in August. Households and businesses that have lived through repeated price surges begin to expect them. Wages, contracts and prices start to build in higher inflation, and at that point the shock is no longer temporary.

> The cost of acting early is a few months of slower growth. The cost of acting late is years of lost credibility.

Central banks learnt this painfully in 2021 and 2022. They called inflation transitory, waited too long and then had to raise rates faster and further than anyone expected. The adjustment was brutal for borrowers, and it would have been gentler had it started sooner. The split on the Monetary Policy Committee, where three of nine members already voted for an increase in September, suggests the Bank has absorbed the lesson. A small, predictable step now is preferable to a larger one later.

What the Bank should avoid is the tone of an institution embarrassed by its own mandate. Price stability is not a technocratic fetish. Inflation hurts most those with the least protection: people on fixed incomes, renters, savers holding cash and workers without bargaining power. A central bank that defends price stability is defending them, and it should say so, plainly and often. Credibility is the cheapest instrument a central bank possesses. When markets and households believe it will do what is necessary, it usually has to do less.

Monetary policy does not act alone. A Budget that adds support for households while the Bank is trying to cool demand makes its work harder. Co-ordination does not mean the Treasury takes orders from Threadneedle Street. It means both sides acknowledge they are pulling on the same economy.

A rate rise into an energy shock will be unpopular. Allowing inflation to settle in would be worse. The Bank should act, explain why, and resist the temptation to apologise for doing its job.
