On a Sunday afternoon in a Cameroonian town, a group of women gathers in a front room. Each brings an envelope. The contributions are counted aloud, recorded in a school exercise book and handed in full to one member, whose turn it is this month. Next month the pot will go to someone else. Nobody signs a contract. There is no regulator, no credit score and no collateral. Default is rare.

The njangi, as it is called in the anglophone regions of Cameroon, has cousins across the continent: the tontine in francophone Africa, the susu in Ghana, the esusu among the Yoruba, the chilimba in Zambia. Economists group them under a dry label, rotating savings and credit associations. The anthropologist Clifford Geertz, writing about them in 1962, called them a "middle rung" in development, a bridge between traditional reciprocity and modern finance. He expected them to fade as banks spread. Sixty years later, they are still meeting.

## A bank made of people

The tontine solves a problem that every financial system must solve: how to turn many small, irregular savings into occasional large sums that can buy a sewing machine, pay school fees or start a business. A bank does this by pooling deposits and lending them out, protected by capital, regulation and the courts. The tontine does it with something cheaper and more fragile. It relies on information and on social sanction. Members know one another, often for years. They know who is ill, who has lost a job, who is about to marry. A member who takes the pot and then stops paying does not merely lose a financial relationship; she loses her standing in the community.

This is finance stripped to its essentials. The word credit comes from the Latin credere, to believe. A loan is a belief about the future behaviour of another person, and every elaborate instrument of modern banking, from credit scores to covenants to collateral registries, is an attempt to manufacture that belief among strangers. The tontine does not need to manufacture it. It begins with people who already believe in one another, and it works within the limits of that belief.

Those limits are real. A tontine cannot grow much beyond the circle of people who know each other. It cannot easily lend for ten years, diversify its risks or protect its members when an entire community is hit by the same shock: a failed harvest, a collapse in commodity prices, an epidemic. Its strength, intimacy, is also its ceiling.

## The lesson institutions forgot

Douglass North, who shared the Nobel prize for his work on institutions, defined them as "the rules of the game in a society", formal and informal. Much development policy since the 1990s has focused on the formal rules: property registries, commercial courts, central bank independence. These matter. But North was explicit that informal constraints, norms, conventions and codes of conduct, often change more slowly and shape behaviour more deeply than the laws written above them.

African finance offers a vivid illustration. In many countries, formal banking reaches a minority of adults, while informal savings groups reach far more. The reason is not only cost or distance. It is trust. A bank is a stranger. Its branch may be far away, its fees opaque, its staff from another region, its loan officers more interested in collateral than in character. Many people's experience of formal finance includes bank failures, frozen deposits and currency devaluations that wiped out savings overnight. Against that history, the women in the front room are a more reliable institution than the bank on the high street.

Francis Fukuyama argued in Trust (1995) that the capacity of a society to form large organisations depends on the radius of trust its members extend beyond the family. Where trust is narrow, economic life organises itself in small, family-based units; where it is broad, large corporations and deep financial markets can emerge. Africa's informal finance is evidence of high trust within communities. Its challenge is to extend that trust outward, to strangers, institutions and markets, without destroying what makes it work.

## When technology met the tontine

Mobile money is often described as a technological revolution. It was also a revolution of trust. M-Pesa succeeded in Kenya partly because it piggybacked on relationships people already had: with the mobile operator whose airtime they bought every day, with the neighbourhood agent they knew by name, with the relatives to whom they sent money. It did not ask people to trust a distant institution. It made the existing web of trust cheaper and faster to use.

Fintech start-ups across the continent have since tried to digitise the tontine itself, with apps that record contributions, automate payouts and build credit histories from repayment records. The best of them understand what they are dealing with. They do not try to replace the group with an algorithm. They give the group better tools: a reliable ledger in place of the exercise book, a secure wallet in place of the envelope, a record of discipline that a bank can eventually read as a credit history.

The worst of them make the opposite mistake. They treat informal finance as a backward practice to be absorbed, and design products for individuals stripped of their networks. They offer instant loans at high interest through phones, assess creditworthiness from data trails the borrower does not understand, and pursue default through public shaming in contact lists. That is not financial inclusion. It is the extraction of value from the very trust that made informal finance work, and it has already provoked regulatory backlash in several countries.

## Building upward from trust

The question for African finance in the coming decades is how to build large, long-term financial institutions on foundations of trust that are local and personal. There is no single answer, but there are principles.

The first is to connect rather than replace. Savings groups can open accounts in their own name, invest their pooled funds in government securities or money market funds, and borrow as groups against their collective record. Several microfinance pioneers built their models on exactly this principle, lending to groups whose members guaranteed one another. The group remains the unit of trust; the institution provides scale, safety and return.

The second is to earn trust slowly and spend it carefully. Every bank failure, every frozen deposit and every predatory loan app sets back the extension of trust by years. Regulators who protect consumers vigorously are not obstacles to financial development; they are its precondition. So are central banks that keep inflation low, because nothing teaches people to distrust money faster than watching it lose value.

The third is to recognise that trust is a form of capital. It takes years to accumulate and can be destroyed in a day. Institutions that treat their customers as partners, that explain their fees, honour their commitments and stand by their clients in difficult times, accumulate it. Those that do not will find that no amount of technology compensates for its absence.

## The front room and the trading floor

It is tempting to see the tontine and the trading floor as opposite ends of a spectrum, one primitive and one sophisticated. That is a mistake. The trading floor in London or New York runs on trust too: trust that trades will settle, that contracts will be honoured, that central banks will act as lenders of last resort. When that trust fails, as it did in 2008, the most sophisticated system in the world freezes in hours. The difference is that the trading floor has built elaborate institutions to make trust among strangers possible, and still finds that they can fail.

Africa's informal finance reminds us that money is, in the end, a social relationship. It remembers who paid and who did not. It rewards those who keep their word. The continent's financial future will be built by those who understand that the envelope passed around a front room on a Sunday afternoon is not a relic of the past. It is the foundation on which everything else must be built.
