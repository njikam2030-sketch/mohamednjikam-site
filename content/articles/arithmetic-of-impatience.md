Keynes's most quoted sentence is also his most misunderstood. "In the long run we are all dead" is usually recited as a shrug, a licence for short-termism. It was nothing of the kind. Keynes was mocking economists who assured the unemployed of the 1920s that markets would eventually restore equilibrium, as if the decade spent waiting were a rounding error. The long run, he argued, "is a misleading guide to current affairs". A theory that is right only eventually is of little use to people who must eat now.

There is no part of the world to which that rebuke applies with more force than Africa. For half a century, the continent has been offered a long run: liberalise and growth will follow, stabilise and investors will come, build institutions and prosperity will arrive in due course. Some of that advice was sound. Much of it was offered with a serenity that only those who do not bear the cost of waiting can afford.

## The demographic clock

Consider the arithmetic. The United Nations projects that by the middle of the century roughly one person in four on the planet will be African. The African Development Bank has estimated that between 10m and 12m young people enter the continent's labour market each year, while only around 3m formal jobs are created. Those are not abstractions. They are the students I have met in Yaoundé and Douala who hold degrees and sell phone credit, the engineers who drive taxis, the accountants who keep the books of a family shop because no firm will hire them.

A demographic dividend is not a prize that falls to young countries automatically. It is an option, and options expire. If the young people of the 2020s and 2030s find productive work, Africa will experience what East Asia experienced between the 1960s and the 1990s: a generation of rising savings, investment and output per head. If they do not, the same numbers that promise a dividend will deliver frustration, migration and instability. The difference between the two outcomes will be decided within a single working lifetime. That is the arithmetic of impatience.

## What the economists already knew

None of this would have surprised the pioneers of development economics. In 1954 Arthur Lewis, the Saint Lucian economist who would later share the Nobel prize, described poor economies as two economies in one: a vast traditional sector where labour was abundant and its marginal product close to zero, and a small modern sector where capital made labour productive. Development, in his account, was the process by which the modern sector absorbed workers from the traditional one, and the speed of that absorption depended on how quickly profits were reinvested.

Lewis's model has been refined many times, but its central intuition has aged well. Growth in poor countries is above all a story of movement: of people moving from low-productivity to high-productivity activities, of farms to factories, of informal trade to formal enterprise. Where that movement stalls, growth may still be recorded, driven by commodity prices or public spending, but transformation does not occur. Much of Africa's growth in the commodity boom of the 2000s was of that kind. It raised incomes without changing what economies produced or how.

A few years later Alexander Gerschenkron, studying the late industrialisers of 19th-century Europe, made an argument that remains under-read in African policy circles. Countries that start late, he observed, do not simply repeat the path of the pioneers at a slower pace. They take a different path, relying more heavily on banks, on the state and on deliberate co-ordination, precisely because they cannot wait for markets to develop the capabilities that early industrialisers accumulated over a century. Backwardness, in his phrase, carries "advantages", but only for those willing to organise to seize them.

## The patience industry

Set against these traditions, much of the advice offered to Africa since the 1980s looks oddly passive. Structural adjustment asked governments to get prices right and step aside. Later, the governance agenda asked them to build institutions first and expect investment afterwards. Each prescription contained truth. Inflation does destroy savings; corruption does deter investors. But the implied sequence, fix everything and then grow, set a bar that no country in history has cleared before it became prosperous. Britain did not have clean institutions when it industrialised. Neither did the United States, Japan or South Korea.

Justin Yifu Lin and Célestin Monga, in Beating the Odds (2017), offered a sharper alternative. Their argument, built on the experience of economies that did manage to jump-start growth, is that poor countries should not wait for the perfect business environment. They should identify industries in which they have a latent comparative advantage, typically labour-intensive manufacturing and processing in which their costs would be competitive if infrastructure and logistics were adequate, and then fix the binding constraints for those industries specifically: power, roads, ports and regulation inside well-run industrial zones. It is a programme of targeted impatience. Rather than reform everything slowly, reform a few things fast, in a few places, for a few industries, and let success spread.

One need not accept every element of that framework to recognise its central virtue. It treats time as a scarce resource. It asks not "what is the ideal policy?" but "what can be done in the next five years with the capacity we actually have?"

## The price of waiting

There is a financial dimension to impatience that economists of development sometimes neglect and that finance professionals understand instinctively. Delay has a cost of capital. A port that opens five years late does not merely postpone its benefits; it forgoes five years of compounding trade, tax revenue and private investment that would have followed. A power plant that is perpetually "under study" imposes a cost on every factory that cannot be built for lack of electricity.

When interest rates were close to zero across the rich world, this cost was easy to ignore, including for those who lend to Africa. That era is over. With long-term borrowing costs above 5 per cent even for Britain, and far higher for most African sovereigns, the price of slow decisions has risen sharply. The same logic that makes slow payments expensive makes slow projects expensive. A continent that pays a premium for capital can least afford to let capital sit idle in unfinished plans.

## Impatience without recklessness

Impatience is not an argument for haste in everything. Africa has seen grand projects launched for prestige, financed with expensive debt and abandoned half-built. It has seen industrial policies that protected inefficient firms for decades. The lesson of those failures is not that the state should do nothing, but that it should do fewer things, measure them honestly and stop those that do not work. Impatience must be disciplined by evidence, and by the willingness to admit error early.

Nor is it an argument for ignoring macroeconomic stability. Inflation and currency crises have destroyed more African savings than any lack of ambition. The point is that stability is a platform, not a destination. A balanced budget in an economy that creates no jobs is not a success. It is a well-managed stagnation.

## A generation's window

The continent's young people are not waiting for economists to settle their debates. They are building businesses on their phones, learning to code from online courses, moving to cities and, when all else fails, moving abroad. Their impatience is the most valuable resource Africa possesses. The task of policy, and of finance, is to give it something productive to build.

Keynes ended his remark with a warning to his own profession: economists set themselves "too easy, too useless a task" if, in tempestuous seasons, they can only say that the ocean will be flat again once the storm has passed. Africa's season is tempestuous, and its storm is a demographic one. Telling a generation that the ocean will eventually be calm is not economics. It is an abdication.
