Two years ago Senegal was one of Africa's most promising borrowers: a stable democracy, a growing economy and oil and gas production about to begin. Today it is negotiating a restructuring of its eurobonds and waiting for parliament to approve the budget on which an IMF programme depends. The speed of that reversal is a warning to every finance ministry on the continent.

The immediate cause was a discovery about the past. An audit commissioned by the new government revealed that debt had been understated under the previous administration. The IMF now puts Senegal's public debt at 132 per cent of GDP at the end of 2024, once the obligations of state-owned enterprises and government guarantees are included. That is far above the figures on which investors had lent.

## Where things stand

IMF staff reached agreement on a three-year programme worth about $2.2bn on 1 September. It still requires approval by the Fund's management and executive board, corrective measures over the misreporting and financing assurances from Senegal's partners. On 6 October the government and IMF staff briefed external creditors, with about $4.8bn of eurobonds in scope of a debt treatment. Moody's cut the sovereign rating to Caa2 at the end of August, a level it associates with expected losses for private creditors of 10 to 20 per cent.

Dakar hopes for a first IMF disbursement as early as November and an agreement in principle with creditors in December. The Fund has not committed to either date. Meanwhile politics has intervened. The parliamentary finance committee rejected the revised 2026 budget, with members of the ruling Pastef party, which holds a large majority, voting against it, and the finance minister, Cheikh Diba, has asked lawmakers to reconsider. Trade unions began a general strike on 21 October.

> A restructuring is a negotiation over credibility as much as over money. Every delay is paid for in the price of the next loan.

## Why it matters beyond Senegal

Investors do not price African sovereigns in isolation. When one borrower discovers hidden debts, the market asks who else might have them, and spreads widen across the region. Countries that have done everything right, including Côte d'Ivoire, which borrowed for 15 years at an effective 5.39 per cent after hedging into euros in February, can find their next issue more expensive because of a neighbour's problems.

That is why the quality of Senegal's process matters as much as the outcome. A restructuring that is transparent, swift and fair to creditors would limit the contagion and show that African governments can deal with debt problems without years of paralysis. One that drags on, as Zambia's and Ghana's did, would reinforce the impression that the continent's debt architecture is broken.

## The politics of adjustment

The rejection of the budget in committee shows the hardest part of any adjustment: persuading a population to accept austerity for debts it did not choose. The government that uncovered the hidden borrowing came to power promising change, not spending cuts. Unions striking against the programme are expressing a frustration that is widespread across Africa, where debt service increasingly crowds out spending on schools, health and infrastructure.

There is no easy way through. Without an IMF programme, Senegal faces a disorderly default and a far sharper squeeze. With one, it must make painful choices quickly. The best outcome would combine fiscal measures that protect the most vulnerable with a debt treatment generous enough to restore sustainability rather than merely postpone the next crisis.

## Lessons for others

Three lessons stand out for finance ministries elsewhere. Transparency is cheaper than discovery: debts revealed by an audit cost far more in lost credibility than debts disclosed at the outset. The liabilities of state-owned companies and guarantees are public debt in all but name, and markets will treat them as such. And the time to strengthen domestic capital markets and local-currency borrowing is before a crisis, not during one.

Senegal still has strong assets: political stability by regional standards, new hydrocarbon revenue and a credible central bank in the regional monetary union. If it uses the coming weeks to secure parliamentary approval and a clear path with creditors, it can turn a crisis of trust into a demonstration of how to repair it. The rest of Africa, and the investors who lend to it, are watching.
