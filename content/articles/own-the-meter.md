For most of the past century Africa's place in the world economy followed a simple pattern. The continent exported raw materials and imported the finished goods, machines and energy that others produced. Value was created elsewhere and paid for in dollars.

Artificial intelligence threatens to reproduce that pattern in a new form. Whether African companies, banks and governments will use AI is no longer in question; they already do. The question is who will own the infrastructure, who will set the price and where the value will be captured.

In Intelligence on Tap I argued that tokens are to intelligence what kilowatt-hours are to electricity. Every task an AI system performs is metered and billed by consumption. As these systems enter accounting, customer service, credit decisions and public administration, the token bill will sit beside payroll and rent in every budget. A utility bill is a permanent outflow. If every African bank, insurer and ministry pays it to providers abroad, in hard currency, AI becomes one more line in the current account deficit. The terms of access, from price to language coverage to data policy, will be set by others.

> Whoever owns the meter sets the terms. Africa cannot afford to be only a consumer of intelligence.

The continent does not start from neutral ground. African economies pay more to borrow than most. Receiving money is dearer too: sending $200 to sub-Saharan Africa costs 8.46 per cent on average, against 6.36 per cent worldwide. Each premium is a transfer of value away from the continent, and imported intelligence paid for in dollars could become the next.

Owning a share of the meter does not mean racing the largest American and Chinese laboratories to build frontier models. That contest is financed with hundreds of billions of dollars, increasingly borrowed. It means investing in the layers that last. Energy comes first, because data centres need reliable and affordable power, and the countries that solve their electricity problem will be the ones that host computing capacity. Connectivity comes next: fibre, cross-border links and exchange points that keep traffic, and value, inside the region. Then skills, meaning engineers and data scientists but also managers and finance professionals who know how to buy, deploy and govern these systems.

These are long-lived assets, and they suit long-term finance from development banks, pension funds, infrastructure investors and local-currency bond markets. They are precisely what deep domestic capital markets ought to be financing.

Few African countries can build this alone. The continent as a whole has scale. A regional approach to data centres, common standards on data protection and a single market for digital services would make investment far more attractive, and the African Continental Free Trade Area offers the framework. Digital infrastructure should be near the top of its agenda.

Africa has missed industrial revolutions before because it was told they were happening somewhere else. This one is being built now, and it runs on electricity, fibre and people. The continent should not merely pay the bill for intelligence. It should own part of the meter.
