John Healey promised millions of people "breathing space". As a statement of intent it is fair. But breathing space in a Budget is never free, and the bill usually lands with someone whose name was not mentioned in the speech.

The way to read this one is the way a finance director reads a contract: not for what it promises, but for what it does to the cash flows.

## A door opens for savers, another narrows

The surprise of the day was a reversal. Last year the previous Chancellor announced that the annual cash Isa allowance would fall from £20,000 to £12,000 for savers under 65. Healey has scrapped the cut, so everyone can keep sheltering up to £20,000 a year in cash.

The relief is real, but it sits beside a measure pulling the other way. From April 2027, tax on savings income and property income rises by a further two percentage points in every band, on top of last year's increases. The message to households could hardly be plainer: shelter your savings or pay more for them. Families will have a stronger reason to use the full allowance every year. For landlords, and for anyone holding large cash balances outside a wrapper, it is a tax rise in all but name.

The Treasury also wants to coax first-time investors into the market with a "Starter Isa", on which the industry will be consulted, though no date was given. A first-time buyer Isa will replace the Lifetime Isa from April 2029.

## A carrot and a stick for entrepreneurs

From April 2027, founders of qualifying high-growth companies will pay capital gains tax at 10 per cent on up to £10m of lifetime gains, with the detail left to a consultation. At the same moment, the lifetime limit for Business Asset Disposal Relief, which lets owners pay 18 per cent when they sell, falls from £1m to £500,000.

> The government is choosing which entrepreneurs it wants to reward: those building scalable companies, not every owner selling a business.

It is a defensible choice, and it will create losers as well as winners. The technology founder with a fast-growing start-up gets a generous rate. The owner of a profitable family firm who sells after 30 years gets less help than before. The consultation will turn into a long argument about what counts as "high-growth" and "innovative", and that definition will decide who actually benefits.

## Relief for the high street, paid by the bookmakers

The threshold at which small businesses start paying business rates rises from £12,000 to £18,000 from April. Healey said 70,000 smaller premises, among them pubs, village halls and corner shops, would pay nothing at all. The cost falls on gambling, through higher duties and the exclusion of betting and vape shops from the relief.

For a small business this is real money. Rates are a fixed cost indifferent to whether the month was good or bad, and removing them for the smallest premises improves the odds of survival just as energy bills begin to climb again.

One measure deserves far more attention than it received. Online marketplaces will become responsible for collecting VAT at source from sellers with turnover of £90,000 or more, wherever those sellers are based. That reaches well beyond Britain. Many small exporters in Africa and Asia sell to British consumers through the large platforms, and VAT compliance among overseas sellers has been patchy. From now on the platform will collect it. British retailers get a level field; overseas sellers who were undercutting them by not paying VAT will find their margins squeezed. Anyone who runs, advises or lends to a business selling into the UK online should model this first.

## Small fixes for a large problem

For households the package is a collection of modest, targeted measures rather than a single headline giveaway. The daily standing charge on gas bills, about £108 a year, will be removed, which should leave three in five households around £45 better off on average, although heavy gas users may pay more through higher unit prices. Some 20 million homes in council tax bands A to D will receive a £100 energy payment, at a cost of roughly £2.6bn. Tariffs come off more than 100 imported foods, worth an estimated £21 a year to the average household. The 5p cut in fuel duty is extended until July, and the National Living Wage rises by about 3.9 per cent from April 2027, from £12.71 to £13.21 an hour.

None of this will change a family's life. Together, it softens a winter in which suppliers have warned of increases of up to 25 per cent in January. The honest description is not breathing space but a smaller squeeze.

There was one line that pointed further ahead: £500m for AI security, alongside a new data centre covenant. Against what American companies are spending, the cheque is small. The signal is not. Britain wants to be a place where artificial intelligence is built and secured, not merely bought, and the covenant will matter more than the money if it speeds up grid connections and planning decisions.

For those affected, the practical consequences arrive quickly. The value of the Isa wrapper has risen, so the allowance is worth using in full. Founders should follow the consultation closely, business owners planning a sale should model the new relief limit before April 2027, and anyone selling through a marketplace should reprice for VAT before the platform does it for them. Households and small firms alike would be wise to keep cash in hand for January.

This is a Budget that redistributes rather than transforms. It protects the gilt market's confidence, leans a little harder on savers, landlords and gamblers, and rewards one particular kind of entrepreneur. For most people the effects will be small. For those in the narrow categories it targets, they will be very real indeed.
