On 29 June, meeting in Yaoundé, the monetary policy committee of the Bank of Central African States cut its main policy rate from 4.75 to 4.5 per cent and lowered its marginal lending rate from 6.25 to 5.75 per cent. It also eased banks' reserve requirements. At its following meeting it left the rate unchanged.

In most of the world, the direction of travel since then has been the opposite. The Federal Reserve raised rates in September. The Bank of England is expected to follow on 5 November. The European Central Bank, to whose currency the CFA franc is pegged, is preparing markets for a possible rise in December. The BEAC, which sets policy for Cameroon, Gabon, Congo, Chad, the Central African Republic and Equatorial Guinea, is swimming against the tide.

## Why the BEAC eased

The decision made sense on the region's own numbers. Inflation in the six economies of the CEMAC has stayed below the community ceiling of 3 per cent; the BEAC's projections for 2026 have ranged between 2.2 and 2.4 per cent. Growth, by contrast, has been slowing: the bank's latest forecast puts it at 3 per cent this year, down from 3.7 per cent in 2025. Credit to the private sector remains thin, and businesses across the region complain that borrowing is scarce and expensive.

A modest cut, combined with lower reserve requirements, was meant to encourage banks to lend more to firms and households. In an economy where small businesses struggle to obtain working capital, that is a legitimate goal.

> A monetary union pegged to the euro can diverge from Frankfurt for a while. It cannot diverge for ever.

## The constraint of the peg

The difficulty is that the CFA franc is fixed to the euro, and the peg depends on adequate foreign exchange reserves. When interest rates in the euro area and the United States rise while those in Central Africa fall, the incentive to hold assets in the region weakens, and the pressure on reserves grows. The BEAC's own projections of reserve cover, which it has put at around four and a half months of imports for 2026, are a key indicator to watch.

Oil adds a complication. Several CEMAC economies, including Gabon, Congo, Chad and Equatorial Guinea, are oil exporters, and Brent above $110 a barrel should strengthen their export earnings and, in turn, the region's reserves. Cameroon, with a more diversified economy, benefits less and feels the higher cost of fuel more directly. The windfall, if governments save rather than spend it, could give the BEAC room to keep its stance. If it is spent, the bank may have to reverse course.

## What it means for Cameroon

Cameroon is the largest economy in the union and the one with most at stake. Its businesses need cheaper credit to grow, its government relies increasingly on the regional bond market to finance its budget, and its households are exposed to imported inflation through fuel and food. A reversal of the June cut would raise borrowing costs just as growth slows. Holding rates while the ECB tightens would widen the gap between Central African and European rates and test the patience of investors in regional government securities.

The deeper issue is structural. Monetary policy alone cannot solve the region's credit problem, which stems as much from weak collateral systems, limited information on borrowers and shallow capital markets as from the level of the policy rate. The most effective response would combine prudent monetary policy with reforms that make it easier for banks to lend: credit bureaus, property registries, enforceable contracts and a deeper regional market for government and corporate debt.

## The decision ahead

The BEAC's next meetings will be shaped as much by Frankfurt and Washington as by Yaoundé. If the ECB raises rates in December, the pressure to follow will be considerable. The bank will have to weigh the needs of slowing economies against the requirements of the peg, and it will have to explain its choice clearly to markets that are becoming more attentive to African monetary policy.

The June cut was a reasonable bet that Central Africa could chart its own course for a while. The coming months will show how long that while can last.
