Central bankers dislike surprising markets, and the Bank of England is unlikely to surprise anyone next week. Almost every signal points the same way: a quarter-point rise in Bank Rate, from 3.75 to 4 per cent, on 5 November.

The decision itself is barely in doubt. What deserves attention is why the Bank feels compelled to tighten into a weakening economy, and what that says about the bind it is in.

## Inflation turns up again

Consumer price inflation rose to 3.5 per cent in September from 3.1 per cent in August, above economists' forecasts. The biggest contribution came from motor fuels, a direct consequence of oil prices pushed up by the conflict in the Middle East.

It is the kind of inflation central banks fear most. Higher interest rates cannot produce more oil. All they can do is restrain demand elsewhere in the economy so that a jump in energy prices does not seep into wages and the wider price level.

The Monetary Policy Committee was already moving. In September it held Bank Rate at 3.75 per cent, but by six votes to three, with the minority wanting 4 per cent at once. When a third of the committee votes to move and the data then go its way, the majority usually follows. Markets have reached the same conclusion: on 28 October, overnight index swaps implied a 97 per cent probability of a quarter-point rise. A Reuters poll in mid-October found 54 of 63 economists expecting 4 per cent in November, and some banks expect a further increase in February.

## A Budget that leans the wrong way

Fiscal and monetary policy can pull together or apart. This week's Budget pulled, modestly, against the Bank.

> Every pound of support for household energy bills is welcome for families. It also keeps demand firmer than it would otherwise be, just as the Bank is trying to cool it.

The support was targeted and the package broadly neutral overall, which is why gilts took it calmly. But its near-term effect is to make the Bank's task slightly harder, and to smooth the path to higher rates even as the OBR cuts its growth forecast for 2027 to 1 per cent.

Nor does the Bank decide in isolation. In September the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75-4 per cent, its first increase since 2023, and most of its officials expect to go further. When the world's most important central bank tightens, others that hold back invite pressure on their currencies and bond markets.

## The case against

There is a serious argument for waiting. Employment has softened and growth is forecast to slow sharply next year. Raising rates into a downturn caused by an external shock risks deepening the downturn while doing little about the shock.

History offers warnings in both directions. In the 1970s, oil price shocks fed into wages and prices for years, in part because central banks responded too slowly and allowed expectations of inflation to take root. The European Central Bank drew the opposite lesson in 2008 and again in 2011, when it raised rates against energy-driven inflation only to reverse course as growth faltered. The Bank of England has to steer between those two mistakes, which is why what it says about wages, services inflation and inflation expectations will matter more than the size of any single move.

Expect, then, a rise delivered in a cautious voice, with heavy emphasis on data dependence. The aim will be to show the Bank takes inflation seriously without committing to a long run of increases.

## What it means in practice

For households coming off fixed-rate mortgages in the next year, monthly payments will be higher than they planned for in 2024 or 2025; new fixed deals already price in tighter policy. Savers will see deposit rates drift up, although the Budget's tax changes will take a larger slice of interest earned outside Isas from 2027. For companies, short-term borrowing costs rise again, and working capital trapped in slow payment chains becomes more expensive, the theme of The Price of Time. Finance directors would do well to assume rates stay higher for longer than last year's plans allowed, to shorten their cash conversion cycles and to consider fixing part of their floating-rate debt while markets are calm. For gilts, one rise is fully priced; the bigger risks for long-dated bonds are persistent inflation and heavy issuance.

Several things could change the picture over the winter. A sharp fall in oil and gas prices would weaken the case for further increases quickly, as would a faster rise in unemployment. Services inflation and wage growth are the best guides to whether inflation is becoming entrenched; if they stay contained, the Bank can stop soon. A disorderly rise in long-term gilt yields would tighten financial conditions on its own and reduce the need for more. And January's energy bills will shape the next move more than any speech.

A rise on 5 November would not mean the British economy is overheating. It would mean the Bank believes it cannot afford to let a supply shock harden into a permanent inflation problem. It is a defensive decision taken in difficult conditions, and the tone that surrounds it will matter as much as the move.
