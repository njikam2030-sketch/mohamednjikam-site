The International Monetary Fund titled its October World Economic Outlook "Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices". The first half of that phrase is what most headlines reported. The second half is what matters.

The resilience is real. The Fund projects global growth of 3.1 per cent this year and 3.4 per cent in 2027, despite an energy shock, higher interest rates and record levels of public debt. The United States is expected to grow by 2.3 per cent in both years, China by 4.5 per cent this year and 4 per cent next, India by about 7 per cent. Against the fears of the spring, when war in the Middle East sent oil soaring, that is a creditable performance.

## Fewer engines

But averages can mislead. The IMF notes that growth has become increasingly concentrated: 32 countries now account for 95 per cent of global expansion, compared with 54 in April. In plain terms, the world economy is being carried by fewer engines. A slowdown in any of them would be felt everywhere.

Part of the explanation is artificial intelligence. Investment in data centres, chips and power has become a powerful source of demand in the United States and a handful of Asian economies, and the Fund's analysis credits it with supporting growth. Countries outside that supply chain do not share in the boom, and some pay for it indirectly through higher long-term interest rates, as AI borrowers compete with governments for the same savings.

> The world is not slowing down. It is splitting, between economies that make the things the boom needs and those that pay for the shock.

## Inflation returns

The second warning concerns prices. Global headline inflation is now projected to rise to 4.7 per cent this year before easing to 4.1 per cent in 2027. For central banks that spent two years declaring victory over the last surge, that is an uncomfortable reversal. It explains why the Federal Reserve has resumed raising rates, why the Bank of England is expected to follow and why the European Central Bank is preparing markets for a possible move in December.

Higher rates in the rich world travel quickly to the rest. They strengthen the dollar, raise the cost of refinancing and draw capital away from emerging markets. Countries with large dollar debts feel the squeeze twice: once through their import bills and again through their interest payments.

## The poorest pay the most

That is why the most important line in the report may be the least reported. Emerging and developing economies most exposed to the higher cost of oil and gas are expected to see growth slow to 3.7 per cent this year, from 4.5 per cent in 2025. Many of them are in Africa. For these economies, the energy shock is not an abstraction: it shows up in the price of transport, fertiliser and electricity, in weaker currencies and in budgets squeezed between subsidies and debt service.

The Fund's prescription is familiar: rebuild fiscal buffers, keep inflation expectations anchored, protect the vulnerable through targeted support rather than broad subsidies, and pursue reforms that raise productivity. All of it is sensible. All of it is harder when growth is slowing and borrowing costs are rising at the same time.

## What it means for policy

The useful reading of the outlook is not that the world economy is fine. It is that its resilience depends on a narrow base, at a moment when inflation, debt and energy prices are all moving the wrong way. That calls for humility from forecasters and urgency from governments.

For African policymakers in particular, the report is an argument for building resilience at home: deeper local capital markets that reduce reliance on dollar borrowing, investment in energy that cuts exposure to imported fuel, and regional integration that creates scale. The global economy will keep growing. The question the IMF poses, without quite saying so, is who will share in that growth and who will simply absorb its shocks.
