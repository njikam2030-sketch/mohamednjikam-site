"Biggest year ever for US IPOs" is a true headline, and it will be repeated often between now and December. Records make good copy. This one conceals almost as much as it reveals.

According to Renaissance Capital, 114 companies had listed in the US by mid-October, raising $147.6bn, more than three times the $44bn raised in the whole of 2025. The previous full-year record, $142.4bn in 2021, fell in August. The second quarter alone, with 48 listings raising $104.8bn, was the largest on record. On those figures the market is booming.

Now take out two deals. SpaceX raised about $75bn, more than half the year's total, and SK hynix added roughly $26.5bn. Between them they account for around two-thirds of everything raised.

> Without its two giants, 2026 would be a good year for IPOs. With them, it is a record. Those are not the same thing.

Meanwhile the number of listings has fallen. Depending on the count, there have been between a tenth and a third fewer US flotations than at the same point last year. More money is going to fewer companies.

## Why the giants are coming now

The largest private companies have chosen this year for reasons that reinforce one another. They need capital on a scale private markets struggle to supply: the businesses building rockets, chips and AI models spend tens of billions of dollars a year, and public markets remain the deepest pool of equity in the world. Their early backers want their money back, since venture and growth funds that invested five or ten years ago owe cash to their own investors and a listing is the cleanest way to return it. And valuations are generous for anything associated with AI or frontier technology. When the price is right, founders sell.

The next test is already on file. Anthropic submitted its registration statement on 26 October without disclosing terms, and expectations point to a raise of about $75bn, on a par with SpaceX. If it goes ahead, 2026 will set a record that may stand for a long time.

## Who is left out

Concentration matters because the IPO market is meant to do more than finance giants. It has historically been where medium-sized companies raised growth capital and where ordinary investors could buy into the next generation of businesses early. That function is weaker than the headline suggests. Mid-sized companies are listing, but in smaller numbers and to a choosier audience, and a business without an obvious link to AI, defence or energy finds the window narrower than the totals imply. Founders and finance directors should not read the aggregate as an invitation. They should read the deals that look like theirs.

The record is no comfort to London, which has spent years trying to win back listings. The largest companies follow the deepest liquidity, and in 2026 that liquidity is in New York. African and Asian companies face the same calculation. New York offers scale and valuation; a listing at home offers proximity to customers and to investors who understand the business, and it helps local capital markets mature. The strongest African exchanges will be built by companies that list at home first and abroad second, not the other way round.

## What earlier peaks teach

Record years deserve respect and suspicion in equal measure. The two previous peaks in US listings, around 1999-2000 and in 2021, were followed by sharp falls both in new issues and in the share prices of many recent arrivals. This year's crop is different in important ways: the companies are larger and more established, on average, than the loss-making start-ups of 2000 or the blank-cheque vehicles of 2021, and many have real revenues and real assets.

But one pattern repeats across cycles. The heaviest volumes arrive when valuations are highest, because that is when sellers are keenest. Investors who remember this become more selective at the top of the market, not less. They will want to know how much of the money raised is new capital for the company rather than shares sold by insiders, what happens when lock-ups expire in 2027, and how this year's listings are trading against their offer prices. A record that ends with most new shares underwater would close the window quickly, and because the biggest deals rest on the same theme, a change in sentiment towards AI would hit all of them at once.

For a founder weighing a listing in the next two years, the record should change the preparation rather than the timetable. Audited accounts, governance and reporting take time to build, and having them ready allows a company to move when conditions suit it rather than when the market is in fashion. A valuation that rests on being "an AI company" will fall when sentiment turns; one that rests on cash flows will hold. A small free float with a large lock-up expiry ahead invites volatility nobody controls, and a listing close to customers can build a more loyal shareholder base than a bigger but distant exchange. Private credit and growth equity remain open to those not ready, or not willing, to accept public scrutiny.

The year 2026 is a record for IPO proceeds and an ordinary year for IPOs. Both statements are true. The gap between them is the difference between reading markets and reading headlines.
